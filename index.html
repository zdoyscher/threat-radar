CONTEXT — WHY "AIs THAT DO THE WORK" IS THE DEEPEST THREAT ON THE BOARD

Most of this radar is about AIs that answer. This one is about AIs that act. For execution-heavy firms — agencies that build the funnels, consultants who produce the deliverables, operators who run the process — the moat was never just knowing what to do; it was that doing it took skilled humans and hours. Agents attack exactly that moat. They plan a goal, browse, call tools and APIs, write and run code, operate software, check their own work, and only surface for a human's approval. This pass gave two very different proofs of how far that has come. The first is the people building these systems openly calling for a slowdown: when Anthropic's CEO writes an essay begging the industry to "pace the frontier," a researcher resigns warning of catastrophe, and OpenAI's CEO says going public would be "ill-advised" because there's too much alignment work left, that is a statement about how capable and unpredictable the technology has become. The second is uglier and more concrete: a criminal actor pointed a swarm of hundreds of agents at the open internet and compromised organizations in dozens of countries faster than any human team could, with the agents planning and adapting largely on their own. Flip the polarity from a safety scare to your delivery pipeline and it's the same machine carrying a complex, multi-step job from start to finish with minimal supervision. That's why this holds the deepest structural position on the board — and why, on a weekend when agents are both the thing the labs fear and the thing that just broke into 400+ networks, it stays #1. The honest caveats still matter (Amodei's essay is a proposal, not a binding pause; the attack's agents "went off script" and only a fraction reached the deepest access), but the trajectory is unmistakable, and it points straight at the hours you bill for.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

The exposure here isn't your answers — it's your doing. If a meaningful share of your revenue is the execution (building the deck, wiring the automation, producing the campaign, running the recurring process), an agent that completes multi-step work end-to-end compresses the hours you bill toward zero. Two responses hold up. First, move from doing the task to owning the outcome: agents execute steps, but they don't carry accountability for the result with this specific client, don't own the judgment about which steps are worth doing, and don't hold the relationship. Reposition your fee around the outcome and the decision, not the labour. Second — and more optimistically — put the agent inside your own delivery: the same autonomy that threatens your margin can expand it, if you run the execution and keep the client, the strategy and the sign-off. The firms most at risk are the ones selling hours of execution as the product; the ones that adapt sell the result, the taste, and the accountability an agent can't own — and use agents to deliver that result faster. Keep the alarm in proportion (the slowdown is a proposal, not a pause; the PaperCut agents needed a human to point them and often stumbled), but don't mistake proportion for safety: this is the capability aimed most directly at execution revenue, it's the only one still accelerating, and this week it stopped being hypothetical.

CONTEXT — THE WORK ITSELF, NOT JUST THE ANSWER

Every other threat on this board commoditizes the answer. Agents commoditize the doing. An agent is an AI pointed at a goal that can then plan the steps, open the tools, browse the web, write and run code, fill in the systems and come back with the task finished — the consultant's “I'll take care of it” turned into software. Today's Anthropic story makes the scale vivid: when a company's own engineers plus their agents are merging thirty thousand changes a day, the bottleneck stops being “can we produce the work” and becomes “can our tooling keep up with how much work the agents produce.” That is exactly the shape of the service business — a pyramid of junior people doing the executional hours while seniors sell judgment. The honest counter-signal still holds: agents clear only a fraction of messy, high-stakes real-world tasks, and the week's rogue-model incidents prove they still need supervision. But the direction of travel, and the money at stake, is no longer in doubt.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

If your revenue is “we do the work for you” — running the ads, building the funnels, managing the pipeline, producing the deliverables, writing the code — an agent that can complete a multi-step job for a fraction of a specialist's fee is direct, structural pressure on your core line, not a side risk. Today's proof makes it concrete: the executional layer that most agencies and consultancies staff up to deliver is precisely what a capable lab now runs as agent-hours, at a volume that breaks ordinary tooling. The value migrates from doing the task to owning the outcome: deciding what should be done, judging whether the agent did it right, and being accountable when it goes wrong. And the week's rogue-model incidents and cyber-agency probe hand you the pitch — clients deploying agents need a human who runs them with guardrails, review and accountability. The firms that win package the judgment and the responsibility around the agent; the ones most exposed are those whose whole offer is labor a capable agent can now attempt.

CONTEXT — “ACCESS TO A SMART MODEL” IS NOT A PRODUCT

For advisory, consulting and coaching businesses, the open-weight threat is simple and structural: if a near-frontier model is free to download and run, then nobody can build a durable business on being the one with access to the smart AI. Today's Reflection Beam drop is the vivid version — a half-trillion-parameter model, Apache-licensed, with a free API and a would-be NVIDIA owner. The takeaway for owners is unchanged: the moat was never the model, and it is getting cheaper to prove that every week.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

If any part of your offer is “we have access to a powerful AI” — reselling API calls, charging a markup for “our AI tool,” or positioning your expertise as privileged access to intelligence — open weights erode the floor under that price. A client who learns that a 501B model is free to download (or that several capable open models are) will ask why they are paying you for access. The defensible move is to stop selling access and start selling the three things a downloaded model doesn't come with: the judgment about which model and approach fits their specific problem, the integration into their real workflow and data, and the accountability for the outcome. Today's independent-benchmark surprise is itself a selling point: clients can't tell which open model is actually good — you can, and keeping that current is a service. The firms most exposed are those whose entire value proposition is “we'll give you AI” rather than “we'll make AI work for your business.”

Climb from building agents to advising on, governing and owning them. The platforms ship the capability; sell the judgment about how to use it in a specific business — before the build line dries up.
  1. Stop selling custom builds the platforms now include. If ServiceNow, GitHub, Salesforce, Microsoft — or a cheap model like Elorian Glimpse — ships it as a feature, “we'll build one” is a shrinking line. Pivot the offer before clients notice they already have it.
  2. Sell “make your agents actually work.” Platform selection, workflow design, guardrails, cost control, change management, adoption and accountability for results — the judgment layer the vendors don't ship (even as they ship the governance tooling, like AI Passport).
  3. Specialize by platform + outcome. “We make Agentforce drive X for home-services firms” beats “we do AI” — depth is defensible where generic build work isn't.
  4. Own the result, not the integration. Tie your fee to whether the agents deliver a business outcome, not to the hours of setup — the setup is becoming a toggle.

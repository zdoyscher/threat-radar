CONTEXT — WHAT "OPEN WEIGHTS" ACTUALLY MEANS FOR A NON-TECHNICAL OWNER

Ignore the parameter counts and the sold-out headline. Here is the only mechanic that matters. Until now, when your client used a cheap AI tool, that tool was renting its intelligence from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google — companies with shareholders, compute bills, and every incentive to raise prices over time. That was quietly your friend: it put a floor under how cheap the substitute for your work could get. Open weights remove the landlord. When Moonshot publishes K3 on July 27, any tool-maker or hosting company can run a top-four model themselves and charge whatever covers electricity — and the fact that Moonshot's own API sold out is irrelevant, because the point of open weights is that nobody has to use Moonshot's servers. You will not see this as "my client downloaded a 2.8-trillion-parameter model" — nobody's client is doing that (the file is ~1.4TB even compressed). You will see it as the $79/month tool in your category quietly getting much better at the thing you charge $4,000 for, and never getting more expensive. DeepSeek already shows the endpoint: V4-Flash runs at $0.14 per million input tokens and $0.28 out. For scale, a 5,000-word strategy document is on the order of a few cents of model cost — and this week Nvidia and Microsoft argued in public that this is the future to embrace, not resist, which is exactly why the story is everywhere right now.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

This threat reaches you indirectly, which is exactly why owners under-rate it. Nobody's client is self-hosting a 2.8-trillion-parameter model; the r/LocalLLaMA consensus is that almost no one can run K3 locally, and its 1.4TB footprint needs 64+ accelerators. The transmission is through the tools. Every content platform, chatbot, research assistant, ad-creative generator and "AI strategist" product in your client's stack buys intelligence wholesale and resells it — GitHub Copilot just added its first open-weight coding model, and it won't be the last. When wholesale collapses toward zero and can never rise again, three things follow. First, the cheap tier gets good: the $20–100/mo tools that already replace a content retainer stop being a downgrade and start being a defensible choice. Second, your competitors' costs fall to yours: any advantage you built on "I have the good model and my competitor doesn't" evaporates — everyone has it. Third, and most uncomfortable: the price of output becomes public knowledge. Your client can look up what a million tokens costs. When the raw material of a deliverable is measurably worth cents, an invoice that prices the deliverable itself becomes indefensible — not because your work is worthless, but because you priced the wrong thing. The firms that survive this sell the decision, the accountability, and the relationship. The ones that don't sell documents in a market where documents are free.

▶ PLAN OF ACTION
Stop selling the output. Start selling the decision about the output.
  1. Price one deliverable in tokens this week. Take your most-produced artifact, generate it with a cheap model, and work out the raw model cost. Seeing "$0.11" next to a line item you bill at $1,200 is unpleasant and clarifying. That gap is your value — it had better be describable as something other than the document.
  2. Move your own delivery onto the cheap tier deliberately. If a $0.14-per-million model handles first drafts, transcription, summarising and research at acceptable quality, your cost of goods falls while your price holds. Do this before a competitor does it and undercuts you with the savings.
  3. Kill volume-based pricing lines. Anything priced per post, per page, per report, or per hour of production is priced against a collapsing input. Convert those to a flat fee tied to an outcome, or a retainer for judgment and accountability — before renewal season, not after.
  4. Watch July 27, then look at your category's tools 60 days later. Don't panic on release day; nothing changes that morning, and the 1.4TB weights mean hosting capacity takes time to spin up. Check in September whether the tools your clients use got noticeably better or cheaper. That's your real early-warning signal, and it costs you one calendar reminder.
  5. Say it before your client does. "The model behind this is nearly free now — here's why you're paying me anyway" is a strong sentence when you say it first, and a terrible position when they say it first.
REALITY CHECKThis card is #1 on velocity, but be careful not to read the noise as the threat — the noise is a symptom, the irreversibility is the threat. The reason it sits at CRITICAL is narrow and specific: open weights are permanent. Every previous price drop in AI was a decision someone could reverse; the July 27 release can't be — a sold-out API today, and even the 1.4TB size, don't change that a free, top-four model lands in 48 hours. There is no tactic that restores scarcity to output; you cannot out-produce free, and you cannot wait it out. The honest response is a repositioning: what you sell has to become the judgment about which output is right, the accountability for the result, and the relationship that survives being wrong once. That is months of work on your offer and your pricing, and it's worth starting in the quarter where you still have leverage. Equally honest the other way: this is a floor-under-inputs story, not an "AI replaces you tomorrow" one. Your clients still won't know what to do with the output. That gap is the business — but only if you actually charge for it.

Distribution is the whole story: the default engine inside the three applications where business deliverables are made, a free download that runs multi-hour projects across Slack, Teams, Gmail, Drive, SharePoint, Salesforce and the open web, and now a phone line and a health record too.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

Fee compression at the root, hitting all three segments at once. The battleground is the mid-market — startups, agencies, and independent operators willing to route work to whatever is cheapest and good enough — and "cheapest" now spans "free on the desktop app" to "already included in the Microsoft 365 licence you're paying for anyway." That's the killer: there is no procurement decision to win, no trial to lose. The buyer-side surveys are unambiguous. SparkToro's State of Digital Agencies found agency owners agreeing AI threatens their business model rose from 44% to 53% in a year, with only 14% calling their pipeline "very healthy." A Typeface survey found 60% of US senior marketing leaders spent less on agencies as a direct result of AI. The field reports are blunt: one agency owner says a client demanded an 80% fee cut because "they could get better results from ChatGPT," and small businesses moving from a full-service retainer ($3,000–8,000/mo) to an AI-first stack ($100–500/mo) are cutting spend 60–85%. At scale: WPP's headcount fell from 108,044 to 98,655 in a year; Omnicom and IPG shed 23,000+ combined; Forrester forecasts 15% of US agency jobs eliminated in 2026. The mechanism is documented — AI compressed the junior-analyst labour that justified pyramid pricing, and firms captured the gain as internal margin instead of passing it through, which is exactly why the client eventually asks for it back.

▶ PLAN OF ACTION
Sell what it can't: accountability, judgment, and the outcome you sign your name to.
  1. Run the substitution test this week. Take your last three deliverables, feed the same brief into ChatGPT (free desktop, Work enabled), and grade the output honestly against what you charged. Whatever it matches is no longer billable on its own — that's your new pricing floor. Whatever it misses is your actual offer, in your client's language.
  2. Reprice from artifacts to outcomes. If your invoice describes documents (a strategy deck, an audit, a content calendar), it is priced against something now free inside Word. Reprice against the result — pipeline, retained revenue, hours returned — which requires you to be measuring the result. Most firms aren't; that's the work.
  3. Pre-empt the AI discount conversation. A third of agencies have already been asked; half expect it. Decide now what your answer is, in writing, before a client asks you live on a call. The strong answer is not "our costs didn't fall" — it's "here is what I'm accountable for, and here is what changes if I'm wrong."

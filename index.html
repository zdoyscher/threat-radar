CONTEXT — WHY THE BROADEST SUBSTITUTE TOOK THE TOP SEAT

ChatGPT is the default way well over a billion people now get an expert-sounding answer, and yesterday it stopped being “just” the answer. For a week this board tracked DevDay as anticipation; on Sept 29 the anticipation resolved into shipped product, and the shape of it is exactly what should worry advice-based businesses. First, the agent: Dots is an always-on assistant that takes on tasks, connects to your other apps, and works independently — described by OpenAI as “an extension of you.” That is a direct nibble at retainer-style engagements built on “we keep an eye on things for you.” Second, the price: GPT-6.1 Sol ships at roughly one-fifth of the flagship's cost ($2 in / $10 out per million tokens vs Astra's $10/$50) at near-flagship quality — the answer layer keeps getting cheaper. Third, and most underrated, the deliverable: ChatGPT Space is a shared team workspace that blends Slack, Notion and Google Drive with agents embedded, and Collaborative Slides & Pages let people and agents co-write presentations and documents (with export to PowerPoint and Google Slides). The strategy deck, the client memo, the project workspace — the artifacts a consultant or agency charges to produce and manage — now have a first-party home inside ChatGPT. Add a new Pro 500 tier at $500/month (25× the Plus allowance) and 1.2 billion weekly users, and the picture is a single product attacking the answer, the ongoing work, and the deliverable simultaneously. Notably, OpenAI held back its newest flagship, GPT-6.1 Astra, on security grounds — a reminder that even the maker is pacing itself — but everything it did ship lands squarely on the work advice businesses sell.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

If clients pay you mainly for knowledge, recommendations, and the documents that carry them — “tell me what to do, and hand me the deck” — DevDay just widened the substitute on three fronts at once. The generic answer is cheaper (Sol). The ongoing “we monitor it for you” retainer has a rival (Dots). And the deliverable itself — the slides, the pages, the shared workspace — can now be produced and managed inside ChatGPT (Space, Collaborative Slides, Pages). The response is the same durable move the whole board demands, now more urgent: sell what the product structurally can't — judgment applied to this client's specific situation, accountability for the outcome, implementation done with them, and the relationship that makes advice land. Package expertise into outcomes and transformations, not information and artifacts, because both just got commoditized. Use ChatGPT Space and Collaborative Slides to widen your own margin — produce the deliverable faster, serve more clients at the same quality — while you move your price onto the parts a model can't own. And treat the always-on agent as a warning about retainers priced on “keeping an eye on things”: re-anchor those on decisions made and outcomes owned, not attention rented.

CONTEXT — WHY “AIs THAT DO THE WORK” IS STILL THE DEEPEST THREAT ON THE BOARD

Most of this radar is about AIs that answer. This one is about AIs that act. For execution-heavy firms — agencies that build the funnels, consultants who produce the deliverables, operators who run the process — the moat was never just knowing what to do; it was that doing it took skilled humans and hours. Agents attack exactly that moat. They plan a goal, browse, call tools and APIs, write and run code, operate software, check their own work, and only surface for a human's approval. A year ago that was a research demo; yesterday it became a complete consumer category. Apple's Siri AI put an agent on every iPhone, Meta shipped Muse, Microsoft rebuilt Copilot around an Autopilot tier, Google turned Search into an agent platform — and on Sept 29 OpenAI, described in pre-event coverage as the last major holdout, shipped Dots. That completeness is the story: there is no longer a “when will agents arrive” question, because they've arrived from everyone. The reason agents slip a place rather than hold #1 is purely about attention — the week-long anticipation of OpenAI's move was itself a velocity driver, and now it's resolved. The structural fact underneath is unchanged and worse: the delivery layer of your business is turning into a line item on someone else's invoice. Cognition's Devin proving companies will pay hundreds of millions a year for an autonomous coding agent (run rate up from ~$492M in May to ~$900M by September), and Microsoft making Autopilot a default Copilot tier, are the commercial half of the same reality.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

The exposure here isn't your answers — it's your doing. If a meaningful share of your revenue is the execution (building the deck, wiring the automation, producing the campaign, writing the code, running the recurring process), an agent that completes multi-step work end-to-end compresses the hours you bill toward zero — and that capability is no longer theoretical or coming: it's a shipped consumer product from every major lab (Dots, Muse, Siri AI, Copilot Autopilot) and a ~$900M product line (Devin). Two responses hold up. First, move from doing the task to owning the outcome: agents execute steps, but they don't carry accountability for the result with this specific client, don't own the judgment about which steps are worth doing, and don't hold the relationship. Reposition your fee around the outcome and the decision, not the labour. Second — and more optimistically — put the agent inside your own delivery: the same autonomy that threatens your margin can expand it if you run the execution and keep the client, the strategy and the sign-off. The category being complete is, oddly, clarifying: there is no more “wait and see.” The tools your clients will use to skip you now exist on every platform; the only question left is whether you've repositioned before they notice.

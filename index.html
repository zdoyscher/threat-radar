CONTEXT — WHAT "OPEN WEIGHTS" ACTUALLY MEANS FOR A NON-TECHNICAL OWNER

Ignore the parameter counts and the spy-thriller headlines for a second. Here is the only mechanic that matters. Until recently, when your client used a cheap AI tool, that tool was renting its intelligence from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google — companies with shareholders and every incentive to raise prices over time. That was quietly your friend: it put a floor under how cheap the substitute for your work could get. Open weights remove the landlord. Now that Moonshot has published K3, any tool-maker or hosting company can run a top-tier model themselves and charge whatever covers electricity. This week added the proof that this is real, not theoretical: Microsoft — the company that helped build the paid frontier — is reportedly testing the free Chinese model to cut its own bill by hundreds of millions. You will not see this as "my client downloaded a 2.8-trillion-parameter model" — nobody's client is doing that (the file is ~594GB and needs data-centre hardware). You will see it as the $79/month tool in your category quietly getting much better at the thing you charge $4,000 for, and never getting more expensive. DeepSeek already shows the endpoint: V4-Flash runs at $0.14 per million input tokens and $0.28 out — a 5,000-word strategy document is a few cents of model cost. The White House theft accusation and the sanctions talk are the sound of the old landlords fighting the eviction; they don't put the weights back in the box.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

This threat reaches you indirectly, which is exactly why owners under-rate it. Nobody's client is self-hosting a 2.8-trillion-parameter model; the transmission is through the tools. Every content platform, chatbot, research assistant, ad-creative generator and "AI strategist" product in your client's stack buys intelligence wholesale and resells it — and this week we learned even Microsoft Copilot may route to an open-weight model to save money. When wholesale collapses toward zero and can never rise again, three things follow. First, the cheap tier gets good: the $20–100/mo tools that already replace a content retainer stop being a downgrade and become a defensible choice. Second, your competitors' costs fall to yours: any advantage you built on "I have the good model and my competitor doesn't" evaporates — everyone has it, including the tools your client already pays for. Third, and most uncomfortable: the price of output becomes public knowledge. When a client can look up that a million tokens costs cents, an invoice that prices the deliverable itself becomes indefensible — not because your work is worthless, but because you priced the wrong thing. The firms that survive sell the decision, the accountability, and the relationship. The ones that don't sell documents in a market where documents are free.

▶ PLAN OF ACTION
Stop selling the output. Start selling the decision about the output.
  1. Price one deliverable in tokens this week. Take your most-produced artifact, generate it with a cheap model, and work out the raw model cost. The second is newer and stranger: Comet's agent visits sites as a stand-in for the user — logging in, navigating, acting — which raises an unsettled question every site owner will eventually face: do you want AI agents transacting on your site on a customer's behalf, and are you even allowed to stop them?

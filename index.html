CONTEXT — WHAT "OPEN WEIGHTS" ACTUALLY MEANS FOR A NON-TECHNICAL OWNER

Ignore the parameter counts and the spy-thriller headlines. Here is the only mechanic that matters. Until recently, when your client used a cheap AI tool, that tool rented its intelligence from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google — companies with shareholders and every incentive to raise prices. That was quietly your friend: it put a floor under how cheap the substitute for your work could get. Open weights remove the landlord. Now that Moonshot has published K3, any tool-maker or hosting company can run a top-tier model and charge whatever covers electricity. You won't see this as "my client downloaded a 2.8-trillion-parameter model" — nobody's client is doing that (the file is ~1.4TB and needs data-centre hardware, and Kimi's own hosted API still charges $15/M out). You'll see it as the $79/month tool in your category quietly getting much better at the thing you charge $4,000 for, and never getting more expensive. DeepSeek shows the endpoint: at ~$0.87 per million output tokens, a 5,000-word strategy document is under a cent of model cost. The White House accusation and the sanctions talk are the sound of the old landlords fighting the eviction — they don't put the weights back in the box.

IMPACT — WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE MONEY

This threat reaches you indirectly, which is exactly why owners under-rate it. The transmission is through the tools: every content platform, chatbot, research assistant, ad-creative generator and "AI strategist" product in your client's stack buys intelligence wholesale and resells it — and last week we learned even Microsoft Copilot may route to an open-weight model to save money. When wholesale collapses toward zero and can never rise again, three things follow. First, the cheap tier gets good: the $20–100/mo tools that already replace a content retainer stop being a downgrade and become a defensible choice. Second, your competitors' costs fall to yours: any advantage built on "I have the good model and my competitor doesn't" evaporates. Third, and most uncomfortable: the price of output becomes public knowledge. When a client can look up that a million tokens costs cents, an invoice that prices the deliverable itself becomes indefensible — not because your work is worthless, but because you priced the wrong thing. The firms that survive sell the decision, the accountability, and the relationship. The ones that don't sell documents in a market where documents are free.

CONTEXT — TWO SEPARATE THREATS UNDER ONE NAME

Perplexity is really two exposures for advisory firms. The first is the answer engine: like Google's AI Mode, it satisfies a research question in place, with citations, so the visit to your site — the article, the comparison, the guide — often never happens. For research-heavy consultants and inbound-reliant firms that's the same zero-click erosion as threat #4, on a smaller but growing surface. The second is the agentic browser: Comet doesn't just answer, it acts — navigating sites, filling carts, comparing options and completing tasks for the user. That's an early look at a web where your client's assistant, not your client, is the "visitor" interacting with businesses — which reshapes how discovery, comparison and booking work, and therefore how anyone reliant on being found and chosen gets found and chosen. Both are live today; what's holding the second in partial check is the unresolved legal question of whether sites can block those agents, which is exactly what the Ninth Circuit is deciding.
